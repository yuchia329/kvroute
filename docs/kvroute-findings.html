Where cache-aware routing stops paying for itself

Six routing policies, five RTX 3090s, one 4-bit Llama 3.1 8B per card. The question was whether a router that models KV-cache residency beats plain consistent-hash session affinity — and where it stops being worth the machinery. It does beat it, by up to +373%. The machinery is worth about a tenth of the win.

The grid’s working-set axis is labelled with what each cell was configured for, not what it applied — and it is off in two directions at once.

Down. Skew discounts working set, because concentrating the draws touches fewer distinct conversations, and a cell of finite length cannot touch a pool bigger than its visit count. Counted from the session column of the 300 s cells rather than modelled, at WS 1 a cell realised 0.95 of its label at skew 0 and 0.49 at skew 1.4; at skew 0 the four labels realised 0.25, 0.95, 1.78 and 2.26. So the top of the axis is much flatter than the labels suggest. (The published map files still carry an older modelled estimate of 0.97 and 0.40 — the measured replacement is in the working tree, uncommitted, at the time of writing. The figures above are the measured ones.)

Up. The generator sizes prompts at an assumed 4 bytes per token and the engines report about 1.66, so in engine tokens every label understates the pressure applied by roughly 2.4×. Read in the engine’s own units the skew-0 column spans about 0.6× to 5.4× fleet capacity rather than 0.25× to 8×.

Neither discount is corrected, because correcting either would rename the workload and refuse every cell recorded under the old name (ADR-0004, ADR-0007). The consequence for reading this report: compare down a column and along a row, never against another grid, and never read a WS label as a literal multiple of capacity.

02 · The headline figure

The pressure map

Memory pressure on one axis, load imbalance on the other, because they are physically different things. Working-set ratio is offered session tokens over measured aggregate fleet KV; skew is the Zipf α of the draw over conversations. Each cell is the goodput delta between session affinity and prefix affinity, closed loop at 32 virtual users, median of three repetitions.

Prefix affinity beats session affinity everywhere except the low-pressure corner, and it beats it most where traffic concentrates. At WS 0.25 / skew 0 the whole working set fits in cache and there is no imbalance to fix; the two are indistinguishable, which is exactly where the spec predicted they would be.

That is not the same as saying cache-aware routing stops paying there — a distinction the project’s own README blurs. At that same cell round robin scores 5.40 and least outstanding 11.23, against 34.37 for session affinity and 32.04 for prefix affinity: every policy that keeps a conversation together is 3.1–6.4× the policies that do not. What stops paying in the low-pressure corner is the extra machinery on top of a sticky hash, not stickiness itself.

The mechanism is not cache reuse. Between the two affinity policies the prefix cache hit rate differs by 0.0–2.2 points at every point of the grid, and per request they recompute prefill within about 5% of each other. What separates them is load: session affinity hashes a conversation onto one replica and keeps it there, so concentrated traffic piles onto one card. Across 23 cells at skew > 0, the busiest replica’s share of requests predicts goodput at r = −0.797 — 34% on one replica gave 24.61 req/s, 65% gave 5.15.

03 · How much residency knowledge is worth

The residency ladder: none → none-but-reads-the-prompt → believed → exact

Four policies differing only in how much they know about what the replicas actually hold. All four were run on one engine configuration, cell for cell on the same grid, with the same block chunking, the same consistent-hash ring and the same tie rotation — so they differ in what they remember and in nothing else.

session affinity none, sticky

Hashes the conversation id onto a ring of 128 virtual nodes per replica. Never reads the prompt. Blind to load by design — there is no escape hatch when the hash lands hot sessions together.

prefix hash none

Hashes the prompt’s leading 16 blocks (1,024 bytes, ~644 tokens) onto the same ring, then adds a load term: cost is inflight + weight × rank. Holds no index and no per-session state. Modelled on OpenAI’s documented pattern.

prefix affinity believed

A trie of believed residency under a measured TTL (56.97 s) and node cap (16,311), longest-chain match, plus a spill rule that declines the match under load pressure. The expensive policy.

exact residency exact

Follows the engines’ own KV cache events over ZMQ instead of a belief, and asks the engine to tokenize every prompt. Forgets whatever it cannot vouch for.

04 · The control that isolates the index

Prefix affinity against OpenAI’s documented routing pattern

This is not a reproduction of OpenAI’s router. prefix_hash is a mechanism inferred from public documentation of “a hash of the initial tokens” plus machine load. The window size, the weighting, the ring and the rank ordering are all decisions made in this project; no number for any of them has been published by OpenAI. The policy is named for what it hashes, not for the lab that documented one.

OpenAI documents routing that depends on “current machine load and available capacity” and “a hash of the initial tokens,” with prompt_cache_key folded into that hash as a disambiguator rather than a pin — their docs are explicit that keys “influence routing; they do not pin requests to a machine or guarantee a cache hit.” None of this project’s five planned policies is that shape, so a sixth was built as a control. It answers the question nothing else in the plan asks: what does tracking belief buy over a content hash that tracks nothing?

What they share

The same 64-byte block chunking and the same chained FNV-1a hash (prefix.Blocks, called rather than copied). The same consistent-hash ring, 128 virtual nodes per replica, the same avalanche, the same tie rotation. A replica leaving moves the same share of the key space under both.

What differs — and it is only this

Prefix affinity keeps a trie of what it believes each replica holds, decaying under TTL and LRU, and matches the longest chain. Prefix hash keeps nothing: it ranks replicas by ring distance from the prompt’s key and pays weight inflight requests per rank step to move off its first choice.

The prefix index buys about a tenth of what cache-aware routing is worth. Against the stateless hash it is worth +5.4% to +25.2%, median +11.0%, at the 11 of 12 points where the two separate. Against session affinity, both beat it by +4.8% to +362%. Reading the prompt is the big win; remembering what you read is the small one.

What the index buys is hit rate, and only where eviction is real. Its three largest hit-rate gaps over the hash are exactly its three largest goodput margins — WS 1 / skew 0 (+25.2%), WS 3 / skew 0 (+20.1%), WS 8 / skew 0 (+17.9%). At WS 0.25, where nothing is evicted and a hash’s assumption that a replica still holds a prefix is simply true, it buys nothing: −6.4%, inside the spread.

That is the index earning its complexity on one specific thing: knowing what has been evicted. A stateless hash assumes a replica that ever computed a prefix still holds it. At WS 0.25 that assumption is simply true and the hash sustains 98%. At WS 8 the hash falls to 61.5% while the index holds 65.5%, because the index has a TTL and a node cap calibrated against the fleet’s own eviction tail and the hash has no model of eviction at all. Under skew the gap narrows again: a handful of hot conversations take most of the draws, they are always recently used and therefore always resident, and guessing they are resident is as good as knowing.

Session affinity has the best hit rate and the worst goodput. Its prefix cache hit rate is higher than the stateless hash’s at all twelve points — and its goodput is lower at eleven of twelve. That reproduces, from a third direction, what Anyscale published (a load-aware router beating consistent hashing on tail latency despite a lower hit rate) and what CacheRoute published (sticky routing taking the highest hit rate and the lowest capacity). Hit rate is not the objective. It is one term traded against balance, and a policy optimising it alone loses.

The weighting between hash and load is the policy

The balance between the two terms is not a tuning detail — it is the axis the policy lives on, so it was swept rather than defaulted. Five weights at WS 1 / skew 1.4, the load point, with a cold fleet between every one.

The load term is an escape hatch, and closing it costs 63.5% of goodput. Goodput peaks at weight 4, +33.1% over routing on load alone, and the peak’s range (28.09–28.90) is disjoint from second place.

It does not fail through the metric you would expect. TTFT improves monotonically as the weight rises, 465 → 374 ms, because concentrating hot conversations where they hash is exactly what a prefix cache rewards. Decode is what breaks: the batch on the winning replica deepens and at weight 32 the median request’s inter-token gap is 27.3 ms against a 24 ms bar it cleared at 12.5 ms four points earlier. Every SLO violation in this sweep is inter-token latency. Not one is TTFT, which never came within 500 ms of its own threshold at any weight.

The window is 16 blocks — 1,024 bytes, bracketed by the workload’s geometry rather than measured: past the 512-byte shared system prompt a third of sessions send identically, and well inside the first turn every later turn resends unchanged. A note on that window in tokens. The project states it as “roughly 644 engine tokens at the measured 1.59 prompt bytes per token.” Two different measured ratios carry that label: 1.59 B/token, from the 2026-09-08 sweep (410,024,191 prompt bytes over 258,235,143 engine tokens), and 1.6576 B/token, which is what every prefix index in every published run was actually calibrated with — logged identically across five router startups, and the figure belief divergence calls “the 1.66 the engines report.” At 1.66 the window is ~618 tokens, not ~644. Nothing downstream turns on the 26-token difference, but the window is stated against a ratio other than the one the index was sized with, and that is worth knowing before either number is reused.

This is also the spec’s claim that “consistent hashing has no escape hatch when the hash lands hot sessions together” — measured as a continuous knob rather than inferred from a comparison between two policies. It is the same escape hatch the spill rule gives the believed index.

05 · The ablation

Does the spill rule earn its place?

The spill rule is prefix affinity’s escape hatch: having found the replica it believes holds the longest leading run of the prompt, it declines that match when the replica is under pressure and sends the turn elsewhere. It has two branches, and only one of them is armed in any published comparison.

Load-imbalance branch armed

Declines a match when the target replica holds more than LOAD_IMBALANCE_FACTOR = 2× the least-loaded replica’s inflight. This is the branch every published number rests on.

Residency branch measured, kept off

Was to decline under memory pressure. As first built it read vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc, which counts blocks held by running requests — kv = 0.02128 + 0.02135 × inflight at r = 0.973 — so on this fleet it was the load branch. #28 rebuilt it on each replica’s own prefix cache hit rate (r = 0.138 with inflight), and #18’s residency arm swept it alone at three marks: it fires in the opposite direction to the load branch up the working-set axis, so the two pressures are separable — and it loses to the load branch at every usable point, by 5–91%. It stays off on that evidence.

A second full arm ran prefix affinity with the rule off across the same grid — same binaries, same bytes cell for cell, each from a cold fleet. This is the cleanest ablation in the project.

Yes. Prefix affinity with the spill rule beats prefix affinity without it at 11 of 12 points, by +5.8% to +105.9%. Turn the rule off and prefix affinity loses 5–51% of its goodput — and at three points it drops below plain session affinity (WS 1 / skew 0: 8.13 against 8.99; WS 3 / skew 0: 5.97 against 9.32; WS 8 / skew 0: 6.51 against 8.83).

The rule is what spreads hot conversations. With it off, prefix affinity serves each hot conversation from one replica at every point — pinned, exactly as session affinity pins it — and tie-breaking has nothing to choose between. With it on, the busiest replica sits at fair share (21–22%) at all twelve points.

How a rule firing on 0.2–2.5% of decisions is worth a doubling: a spill sends one turn of an overloaded conversation to another replica, which then caches it too. From then on that conversation’s prefix match ties across every replica holding it, and the tie goes to the least loaded. The spill makes the choice once; tie-breaking takes it from there. It does so without costing cache hits — with and without the rule, hit rates match within 1.0 pp everywhere but one point.

Where the rule costs, and where it is simply wrong

  • WS 0.25 / skew 0: spill on is 8.9% below spill off (equivalently, spill-off is +9.7% above spill-on — the two are different numbers and the repository’s README reports the first under the second’s wording), with a hit rate 2.3 pp lower. There is no imbalance to fix and the whole working set fits in cache, so every spill only sends a turn to a replica that does not hold it. This is the one cell in the whole grid where the rule is a net loss — and it is the same cell where the believed index stops beating a plain sticky hash.
  • Placement matters separately from spilling, in both directions. Prefix affinity sends a new conversation to the least-loaded replica; session affinity hashes it. With spill off, that placement is the only difference between them — and it helps under skew (WS 3 / skew 1.4: 6.44 → 21.81) but hurts at skew 0 past WS 0.25 (WS 3 / skew 0: 9.32 → 5.97), where spill-off prefix affinity’s busiest replica runs at 28–31% against the hash’s 22–26%. Why the placement unbalances there is not isolated. The rule rescues exactly those cells: spill on is 64–94% above spill off at WS 1, 3 and 8 at skew 0.
  • Spill-off is also bistable; spill-on is not. At WS 1 / skew 1 and WS 3 / skew 1.4 one of spill-off’s three repetitions fell to half the others — 10.55 and 10.78 against about 21. Spill on showed no such repetition anywhere on the grid.
  • The rule is tuned for one load and misfires at another. At 6 req/s open loop it fired on 19–20% of later turns, against 0.674% at the 32-user rung it was settled at. 42.0% of spilled turns missed the SLO, with 11.9% of prompt cached and TTFT p50 970 ms, against 7.6%, 73.9% and 372 ms for honoured matches. That is why the recovery curves use the spill-off arm.
  • “Twice the fleet minimum” was never a ratio. Across 2,361 weighed decisions the fleet’s minimum inflight was 0 on 96.4% and 1 on the other 3.6% — never once above 1. So a factor of 2 was the constant 2 for the entire run: “decline any replica holding more than 2 requests.” The settled rung has that condition on 16% of its decisions; this rung has it on 100%.

Why, and it is not about the driver. At 32 virtual users the replicas are saturated, so moving a request off a buried replica buys back more queueing than the prefill costs, and the rule is worth +80%. At 6 req/s each replica holds 1.2 requests at the median — six across the fleet, against the 32 the settled rung holds — so nothing is queueing, and a declined match pays a full prefill to escape a wait that was not happening. The rule is not mistuned there. It is answering a question the fleet is not posing.

So the honest form of the answer: prefix affinity + spill beats prefix affinity alone wherever the fleet is loaded enough for queueing to exist, and loses to it wherever it is not.

Exact residency also reaches a lower hit rate than the belief it replaced — 0.8973 against 0.9088 — and makes the engines compute 12.7% more new tokens per request. Its predictions were near-exact, so this is placement, not accuracy.

Knowing what is cached loses to remembering what was sent. The index records what it sent — intent — so several requests arriving at once that share a new prefix are herded onto one replica and the prefix is computed once. Exact residency records only what an engine has finished computing and reported: while the first request is still prefilling, the others are told, correctly, that no replica holds that prefix yet. So they scatter, and the same prefix is computed on several replicas at once.

How this sits against llm-d. llm-d measured P90 TTFT 0.54 s precise against 31.1 s approximate; here the two are 734 ms exact against 458 ms approximate — the other way round. On five consumer cards serving 448-token turns both terms invert at once — the miss is cheap, because a believed index already reaches 0.909 hit rate, while 26 ms is a quarter of the entire TTFT budget. The cost of knowing is roughly fixed; the value of knowing scales with what a mistake costs. This fleet is small enough that the two cross over.

The one honest caveat is that 26 ms is the cost of this way of knowing, not of exactness itself. A router that tokenized in-process would pay perhaps 1–2 ms — rejected in ADR-0010 as needing cgo or an unofficial chat-template port, and as failing silently when a template byte drifts. Even granting a free tokenizer, the 12.7% redundant prefill and the 1.15 pp hit-rate deficit remain, and those are what would still have to be answered. They are the in-flight gap, and llm-d has a knob aimed at exactly it: optional speculative indexing, which also records what the router just sent for 2 s — the belief, layered on the facts. Exact residency here has nothing like it, so what was measured is that an event-fed index without that layer loses; an exact index with it is not measured here.

The hardware, measured rather than assumed

GPU 3 throttles on heat

SwThermal in 67% of samples against 0–34% for the others; clocks to 960 MHz at 75 °C while an 83 °C card holds 1,305 MHz. A permanently slow card is a permanent load imbalance — which is the mechanism under test, paid unequally by load-aware and load-blind policies. Excluded; the fleet is five cards.

Replica symmetry is load-dependent

Driven one at a time the replicas agree to 1.5–2.3%; driven all six at once the spread is 12.7% and grows with time on load. The spec’s symmetry check drives them one at a time — the one configuration in which the effect cannot appear.

Disaggregation is not ruled out, but the margin is six times thinner than the spec assumed

Moving a 2,048-token request’s KV costs 36.7 ms against 490.7 ms of prefilling it: 7.5%, where the spec predicted ~20 ms against a ~1.5 s prefill, or 1.3%. Prefill is three times faster than assumed, and a token’s KV is 131,072 bytes exactly, read off the model config. No pair of cards can reach another directly — the driver reports peer access unsupported on all 30 ordered pairs — so every copy bounces through host memory at about 7.9 GB/s; CUDA’s own peer copy is slower than bouncing by hand (15.6% of prefill against 7.8%). Nothing was built; the arithmetic settled it.

And the two ideas pull against each other. Once prefix-cache-aware routing is working, a hit means only the 512 new tokens need prefilling — 124.1 ms — so the same 36.7 ms transfer is 30% of the work it would save, not 7.5%.

The engine is at the roof

Nsight roofline on an RTX 3090: measured ceilings 844 GB/s and 62.0 TFLOP/s against a 936 GB/s and 71.0 TFLOP/s datasheet, ridge 73.5 FLOP/byte. Prefill runs at 95–100% of the measured compute roof at every shape from 128 to 2,048 tokens; decode is bandwidth-bound at 87–92% of the memory roof up to batch 16 and crosses to compute-bound at batch 32. There is no headroom a better kernel would find — which is why routing is the lever.

09 · Findings in the same voice as the positive ones

Null, negative and withdrawn results

  • The index and its spill rule do not pay at WS 0.25 / skew 0. Prefix affinity is 6.8% below session affinity there (32.04 against 34.37), inside the run-to-run spread — the two ranges overlap. With the whole working set in cache and no imbalance to correct, every spill only moves a turn to a replica that does not hold it: spill-off scores 35.16, +9.7% above spill-on, with a hit rate 2.3 pp better. Two corrections to how this is stated in the repository’s own README: it reports the spill-off arm as “8.9% higher,” which is the reciprocal — 8.9% is how far spill-on sits below spill-off, and the two are not the same number. And it files this under “cache-aware routing does not pay,” which is too broad a scope: at this cell the best score of all (35.16) is itself a cache-aware arm, and the cache-blind policies score 5.40 and 11.23.
  • The first memory-pressure branch measured load, and the one that replaced it is harmful. The spill rule’s KV branch read vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc, which counts blocks held by running requests, so on this host it was the load branch in other units and stayed off for every map cell. Rebuilt on each replica’s prefix cache hit rate, and swept alone by #18’s residency arm, it showed the two pressures are separable — the two branches fire in opposite directions up the working-set axis — and it lost to the load branch at every usable point at every mark. Declining a match because a replica is evicting sends the request somewhere that never held it, which lowers the hit rate and fires the rule again. It stays off.
  • An earlier headline was an artefact and is withdrawn. The first two-policy comparison, on six cards, concluded that balancing load cost goodput — least outstanding losing 16–32%. GPU 3 throttles whenever all six cards draw power at once, so round robin was feeding a sacrificial queue that passed nothing while the others passed everything. On five symmetric cards the same pair inverts and least outstanding wins by 100–120%.
  • The column that was supposed to show imbalance recorded nothing, for the life of the policy. Every session_affinity row written before commit 9311e68 carries inflight 0 — the hash ring stored candidates rather than replicas and is rebuilt only when the replica set changes, so the load it reported was frozen at whichever request first built it, on an idle fleet. Nothing routed differently, because the hash weighs no load by design, so every published comparison stands. What was lost is the evidence for the balance half of the spec — recovered only because the per-request rows name the replica that served each request: counting those at 64 users gives session affinity a busiest replica on 24.6–26.6% of requests against a 20% fair share, where round robin sits at 20.0–20.2%.
  • Redundant prefill compared absolute tokens, so in a closed loop it rewarded the slower policy. A faster policy offers more prompts in the same window, so a column of totals credits whoever served less. Every such figure is now per request, with the token total printed beside it only so the two can be told apart.
  • The index’s decay curve was withheld once, and then published. Every cell of the first recency run flagged as still warming up — precisely the condition that manufactures under-prediction — so the curve was not published. Re-run at a geometry whose cells do not flag, it holds.
  • The characterization run itself is flagged, and everything scales off it. Replica symmetry is unresolved at concurrency 32: a 22.1% TTFT p50 spread over an 8.0% tolerance, and one replica varying 36.3% against itself between repetitions — so the difference between replicas is inside the measurement’s own noise. Four of 36 probes flagged for warm-up drift. The numbers are used anyway, with the flag carried.
  • The comparison this project runs has already been published at datacentre scale, by Anyscale, CacheRoute and llm-d, and saying otherwise would be wrong and instantly caught. What is left is narrower and it is what the project claims: the comparison at single-host consumer-GPU scale; a two-axis pressure map over measured fleet KV crossed with Zipf skew; and belief divergence, which nobody publishes at any scale.
10 · How the project got here

The ticket story

Thirty-three tickets in five acts. The shape is not accidental: a frame written before any code, a tracer bullet that failed on first contact with real hardware and was supposed to, a ladder of policies each built to isolate one thing, a grid that measured them, and a long tail of corrections — most of which took something away from the project’s claims rather than adding to them.

ACT I
The frame, and the restart
#1–#7 · 2026-09-06
#1
KV-cache-aware inference router: v1 open epic
The constitution, not code. A 6× RTX 3090 host and a claim that could not be supported. The corrections
#25, #27–#33 · 09-11 → 09-13

Eight tickets, and every one of them took something away. This is the half of the project that decides whether the other half can be believed.

#25
GPU 3 throttles thermally
The largest turning point. A permanently slow card is a permanent load imbalance — the exact mechanism under test, present in every measurement taken so far and paid unequally by load-aware and load-blind policies. It changed the fleet from six cards to five, forced capacity to be re-measured, and forced the withdrawal of a published headline. The deficit also grows with time on load (1.1% → 12.7% → 17.2% across three repetitions over ~3 minutes), so driving the replicas one at a time — the spec’s own symmetry check — is the one configuration in which it cannot appear.
#27
Session affinity recorded inflight 0 on every row
For the entire life of the policy. The ring stored a candidate carrying load as well as identity, and rebuilds only when the replica set changes — so it reported whatever load its replica had when the first request built it, on an idle fleet. Routing was unaffected, because the hash weighs no load by design, so every published comparison stands. The fix makes the staleness unrepresentable rather than merely fixed, and imbalance is now counted from the served rows on every cell rather than read off the router’s own bookkeeping.
#28
The KV spill condition is measuring load, not memory pressure
Retired the project’s stated premise that the two pressures were separable in the results, downgraded #16’s KV axis from a result to evidence, and forced a replacement signal to be built and priced rather than a threshold to be re-tuned. Two of three swept levels were arithmetically unreachable — 0.85 needed inflight ≥ 38.8 against a fleet of 32 users. The replacement reads the share of its own claims each replica honours: r = 0.138 with inflight, against r = 0.979 for the gauge it replaced.
#29
Re-run belief divergence’s recency axis on a warmed fleet
Converted the project’s most exposed withheld result into a published one. Drift went from +0.936 / +2.311 / +1.933 to −0.079 / −0.115 / −0.145, with the measured window spanning exactly 2.00 visit periods on every cell; 18 of 18 clean, no flag of any kind. The decay is real. Its first design pass also found that the acceptance criterion it had been given was wrong, which is what opened #33.
#30
Redundant prefill compares absolute tokens
So in a closed loop it rewarded the slower policy. Session affinity has the lowest absolute recomputed prefill at all 12 grid points but the lowest per-request figure at only 6, while prefix affinity served 1.2–2.3× the requests at 11 of 12. Two published verdicts inverted. It turned the headline from “prefix affinity wins goodput but looks wasteful” into “prefix affinity wins goodput and wastes least.”
#31
The spill rule fires on a fifth of turns at low open-loop load
19–20% of later turns at 6 req/s, against 0.674% at the rung the factor was settled at. Spilled turns missed the SLO 42.0% of the time with 11.9% of prompt cached and TTFT p50 970 ms, against 7.6%, 73.9% and 372 ms for honoured matches. Both proposed repairs were measured and both rejected; the rule was bounded instead. It converted a tuning question into a scope bound: the factor is not mistuned at that rung, it is answering a question the fleet is not posing, and no setting of either axis there beats declining nothing.
#32
Every run target needs a Go toolchain, so none of them run on the fleet host
Modest but real. The most load-bearing file in the project — the sweep library holding the frozen values compare hard-checks — lived only on an untracked box. Closing this made “a run on the box can be reproduced from the repo alone” true rather than aspirational.
#33
The warm-up drift check is one-sided, splits on the wrong window, and is blind to the workload’s period closed
The last correction, and the one that re-judged everything before it. Three located defects: the flag tested only for a slowing first half, so #29’s think75 cells recording a second half 2.3× slower than the first were published carrying no flag; the split was taken on the wrong window; and the check was blind to the workload’s own period. The rebuilt check is two-sided, compares each turn index with itself and names its cause. All 744 recorded cells were re-judged from their own rows, with no GPU, and every one reproduced its record. Its biggest decision: an open-loop cell past the knee is flagged past saturation and keeps its goodput, or the collapse the open-loop table exists to show would have become a gap. What moved: the map’s WS 3 / skew 1 (+41.3% → +51.4%), exact residency’s WS 1 / skew 1 (now a hole), five points of the residency arm, and the three-policy sweep at 256 users.

What is still open, and what the project does not claim

#18 — pressure grid closed

Closed 2026-09-13 with all seven criteria met. The map stands on 144 cells, the mechanism on a 36-cell spill-off arm, and separability on a 108-cell residency arm. Its open questions are not criteria: why least-loaded placement of a new conversation unbalances an even draw, and whether a residency rule with a different target would work.

#33 — warm-up drift check closed

Closed 2026-09-14. Every recorded cell now carries the rebuilt check’s verdict, and the figures in this report are the re-judged ones. Two known limits were kept by decision: a cell is called past saturation only when its latency also moved, and the 20% backlog line sits in a narrow gap (16% below, 28% above). The re-score report prints each cell’s backlog, so a cell in the gap will show.

Separability — answered

Measured, not blocked. The engine’s KV gauge reads the running batch, so the branch was rebuilt on each replica’s prefix cache hit rate and swept alone by #18’s residency arm. It fires three- to tenfold more up the working-set axis while the load branch halves: two pressures, not one. It is also harmful at every mark, which is why it stays off.

#1 — the epic open

The only open ticket, and all 19 of its sub-issues are closed. Five of its six policies were measured on the fleet; the sixth, prefix_hash, was outside its five and was measured anyway. One check it names was never run: the router’s derived session identity is built and unit-tested, but every cell sent X-Session-Id, so the sensitivity check that session affinity’s baseline was not handicapped by a perfect oracle does not exist.

One promised table that does not exist

The spec asked the spill rule’s thresholds to be justified by a swept grid — “a second results table, and where the affinity-versus-balance tradeoff becomes a curve rather than an assertion.” #16 ran that sweep: three KV high-water levels (0.70 / 0.85 / 0.95) at WS 3 / skew 0 and 32 users, 13,658 router rows. No results table for it exists anywhere in the repository. The run has no measurement directory; its only surviving trace is the correlation that killed it — kv = 0.02128 + 0.02135 × inflight, r = 0.973 — quoted in ADR-0011 and repeated as a footnote in four generated pressure maps. Two of the three levels were later shown to be arithmetically unreachable at that rung.

So the KV branch is known only by its own refutation. There is no goodput figure for any of its three levels, and the affinity-versus-balance curve the spec asked for exists only on the load axis, where #18’s pre-run factor sweep supplied it. That is still a real hole in the record for the gauge #16 swept. Its replacement is not in the same state: #18’s residency arm priced the rebuilt branch at three marks across all twelve points, and every mark lost to the load branch wherever it had a usable cell, by 5–91% — so the memory half of the rule now has a measured answer, and the answer is to leave it off.

What the project does not claim

  • It does not claim the mechanism. Cache-aware routing is an active production pattern across SGLang, NVIDIA Dynamo, llm-d, AIBrix, Mooncake, MiniMax-M2 and OpenAI, and the field is convergent: every one of them scores replicas by predicted prefix reuse and penalises by load. Policy 4 is that same shape.
  • It does not claim the ablation. Anyscale benchmarked a consistent-hash router against a KV-aware one and found the KV-aware router best on p99 despite a lower hit rate; CacheRoute reached 176 ± 11 QPS against sticky hashing’s 30 at a 3.5 s p99 SLO on 60 H100s, with sticky taking the highest hit rate of its baselines; llm-d publishes a numeric threshold at which it abandons stickiness. All three landed during this project’s own research window.
  • It does not reproduce OpenAI’s router. prefix_hash is inferred from public documentation. The 16-block window, the weight of 4, the ring and the rank ordering are decisions made here, and every write-up of that comparison says so in the same sentence that names the inference.
  • What is left, and is claimed: the comparison at single-host consumer-GPU scale, where a working set can be pushed past fleet capacity deliberately and the crossover sits somewhere else entirely; a two-axis pressure map over measured aggregate fleet KV crossed with Zipf skew; and belief divergence — the per-request gap between a router’s model of cache residency and the engine’s own account of what it computed — which nobody publishes at any scale. Also new, and smaller: a spill threshold whose firing rate depends on load (0.674% of later turns at 32 users, 19% at 6 req/s open-loop, where llm-d publishes one fixed threshold); what an index buys over a load-weighted prefix hash (+11.0% median); and where recovery costs land — after a replica kill for session affinity, during it for prefix affinity.
  • It reproduces, rather than discovers, the two findings Anyscale and CacheRoute already published: the win comes from load balance rather than extra cache hits, and pure stickiness takes the highest hit rate and the lowest capacity.
  • It does not yet test the strongest cheap baseline. Session affinity here has no load bound. Envoy (hash_balance_factor) and HAProxy (hash-balance-factor) turn on consistent hashing with bounded loads with one setting, and CacheRoute uses it as a baseline. Since the mechanism is load, every margin on this page is against load-blind stickiness, not against the best a plain load balancer offers. Putting that policy on the pressure grid is the test of whether the index or just a load bound carries the +66%.

The answer to the question on the masthead. Cache-aware routing stops paying for itself — against plain consistent-hash session affinity — when the working set fits in cache and the traffic is uniform. At WS 0.25 / skew 0 prefix affinity is 6.8% below that baseline, inside the spread. Everywhere else it pays, by +21% to +373%. Note the baseline carefully: session affinity is itself a locality policy, and against routing that ignores locality entirely it wins at that corner by 3.1–6.4×.

But the expensive part is not what pays. Reading the prompt at all — a hash of its leading blocks onto a ring, weighed against inflight, holding no state — captures +4.8% to +362% of that. The trie, the TTL, the node cap, the calibration and the divergence measurement that check it add a further median +11.0%, and only where eviction is real. And a valve firing on 0.2–2.5% of decisions is worth more than all of the belief-tracking above it.

Provenance

How this report was checked

Every table here is transcribed from the measurement files in docs/measurements/ rather than from the project README, and ten of the headline claims were independently re-derived from the raw per-cell records — medians recomputed from cells.parquet and the per-cell JSON, repetition ranges checked for overlap, flagged cells checked for inclusion, and denominators checked against the row counts.

A separate pass audited the repository for measured results that exist in the files but appear in no write-up. It found three, and they are reported where they belong: the twelve per-point spill-off cell tables (aggregated in §05), the router’s worst-case dispatch column, and #16’s KV-axis sweep, which has no measurement directory at all (§11). It also found one uncommitted change in the working tree that supersedes a published number — the working-set axis realisation, corrected in §01.

Nine of the ten claims held exactly. One did not, and the correction is carried in §09: the README reports the spill-off arm at WS 0.25 / skew 0 as “8.9% higher,” which is the reciprocal of the figure in its own table — spill-off is +9.7% above spill-on, and spill-on is 8.9% below spill-off. The same bullet files the result under “cache-aware routing does not pay,” where the cell’s own top score is a cache-aware arm. Two further discrepancies surfaced and are noted where they matter: the session-affinity column differs between the ladder and the map by whether three warm-up-flagged cells are included, and the headline map and its earlier draft differ at WS 1 / skew 1.4 (+171.2% final against +179.5%), where this report uses the final.
